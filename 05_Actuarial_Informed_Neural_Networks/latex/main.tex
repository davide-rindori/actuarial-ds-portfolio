\begin{abstract}
We introduce Actuarial-Informed Neural Networks (AINN), a constrained deep learning framework that embeds actuarial domain knowledge directly into the LSTM training process through differentiable penalty terms. Applied to sex-specific multi-population longevity forecasting for a 6-country high-longevity cluster (1956--2020), the framework addresses a fundamental tension in neural mortality modeling: unconstrained networks optimize for one-step-ahead accuracy but may sacrifice long-horizon forecasting stability.

Our central finding is that the monotonicity constraint intensity $\lambda$ functions as a \textit{continuous governance dial} with a quantifiable accuracy-stability trade-off. At $\lambda = 0.01$, confidence intervals narrow by 6.5\% at zero RMSE cost. At $\lambda = 0.1$, they narrow by 25\% at a cost of only +0.84\% RMSE. This trade-off is invisible to standard hyperparameter optimizers, which select $\lambda$ by minimizing one-step RMSE --- the wrong metric for evaluating structural constraints.

The framework incorporates three additional methodological contributions: (i) residual-calibrated process noise that eliminates double-counting in the dual uncertainty framework, reducing confidence intervals by 53--60\%; (ii) a 5-seed model ensemble that achieves a cross-validation coefficient of variation of 1.06\%; and (iii) a time-varying credibility weight $Z(t) = e^{-\alpha t}$ that provides a principled mechanism for blending neural near-term predictions with actuarial long-term priors, connecting to B\"uhlmann credibility theory.

A 10-year out-of-sample backtest (trained on 1956--2010, validated on 2011--2020 observed HMD data) yields a mean absolute error of 0.38 years for life expectancy, with 11 of 12 country$\times$sex combinations falling within the 90\% confidence interval. The model produces regulatory-grade outputs including SCR calibration under SST (ES 99.0\%: +1.91 years, CHE Male) and Solvency II standards, reverse stress testing, and a model-based stability test confirming no explosive amplification (ratio 1.02$\times$).

\vspace{0.4cm}
\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} Actuarial-Informed Neural Networks, Constrained Deep Learning, Multi-population Longevity, Sex-specific Mortality, Credibility Theory, Swiss Solvency Test (SST), Model Governance.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

The accurate quantification of longevity risk is a prerequisite for capital adequacy under the Swiss Solvency Test (SST) and Solvency II frameworks \cite{swissre2022}. Classical stochastic mortality models --- primarily the Lee-Carter \cite{leecarter1992} and Li-Lee \cite{lilee2005} frameworks --- have served as the industry benchmark for decades. The emergence of actuarial machine learning \cite{wuthrich2021statistical, wuthrich2023} has demonstrated that neural architectures can capture non-linear mortality dynamics that linear models miss, with recent contributions by Richman and W\"uthrich \cite{richman2021}, Perla et al.\ \cite{perla2021}, and Nigri et al.\ \cite{nigri2019} showing that LSTMs and feed-forward networks can extend classical actuarial decompositions.

In a companion paper \cite{rindori2026p04}, we introduced Hybrid-Lift, a neural-actuarial framework combining Hierarchical LSTMs with Mean-Bias Correction (MBC) for multi-population mortality forecasting. That work demonstrated selective superiority over Li-Lee (+17.4\% RMSE in Sweden, +12.6\% in West Germany) but left three gaps: (i) actuarial constraints were verified \textit{post-hoc} rather than embedded in training; (ii) the model produced only both-sexes-combined projections; and (iii) process noise calibration included double-counting of the variability already captured by the neural network.

This paper addresses all three gaps by introducing \textit{Actuarial-Informed Neural Networks} (AINN), inspired by the Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINN) paradigm \cite{raissi2019pinn}. The key insight is that actuarial constraints should not be binary (present or absent) but \textit{parametric}: the constraint intensity $\lambda$ is a continuous variable that the risk manager tunes based on the regulatory context. This framing yields the paper's central result --- a quantified governance-accuracy trade-off that no classical actuarial model and no unconstrained neural network can provide.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Constrained Loss Function.} Differentiable penalties for Li-Lee coherence and temporal monotonicity are embedded in the LSTM training loss. We demonstrate that at $\lambda_{mono} = 0.01$, the constraint is a ``free lunch'' (CI $-6.5\%$, RMSE $-0.03\%$), while at $\lambda_{mono} = 0.1$ it produces a meaningful governance-accuracy trade-off (CI $-25\%$, RMSE $+0.84\%$). This intensity analysis is the first systematic parametric study of actuarial constraints in neural mortality models.

    \item \textbf{Residual-Calibrated Process Noise.} The dual uncertainty framework combines MC Dropout \cite{gal2016dropout} with process noise calibrated on the model's \textit{walk-forward residuals} rather than on historical Li-Lee variability. This eliminates double-counting, reducing 95\% confidence interval widths by 53--60\%.

    \item \textbf{Sex-Specific Joint Training.} A single LSTM trained jointly on Male and Female mortality (with a binary sex indicator) doubles the effective sample size and produces coherent sex-specific projections --- a capability absent in our prior work and necessary for L\&H product applications.

    \item \textbf{Time-Varying Credibility MBC.} We extend the Mean-Bias Correction by introducing a time-decaying credibility weight $Z(t) = e^{-\alpha t}$, connecting to B\"uhlmann credibility theory \cite{buhlmann1967}. This provides a principled mechanism for transitioning from neural predictions (strong near-term) to actuarial priors (stable long-term).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Data and Cluster}
% ============================================================

We use the same Frontier Mortality Cluster as \cite{rindori2026p04}: Switzerland (CHE), Sweden (SWE), Norway (NOR), West Germany (DEUTW), the Netherlands (NLD), and Japan (JPN), with data from the Human Mortality Database \cite{hmd2023} for 1956--2020. Unlike the companion paper, which models both-sexes-combined mortality, we extract separate Male and Female mortality matrices $m_{x,t,i,s}$ for ages $x \in [0, 90]$, years $t \in [1956, 2020]$, countries $i$, and sex $s \in \{M, F\}$.

The Li-Lee decomposition is applied independently per sex:
\begin{equation}
    \ln(m_{x,t,i,s}) = \alpha_{x,i,s} + B_{x,s} K_{t,s} + b_{x,i,s} k_{t,i,s} + \varepsilon_{x,t,i,s}
\end{equation}
where all parameters carry a sex subscript. The stationarity analysis (ADF + KPSS) reveals a \textit{fundamentally different landscape} for the sex-specific decomposition compared to the both-sexes-combined analysis: Switzerland Male is stationary (both tests agree, $p < 0.001$), whereas in \cite{rindori2026p04} Switzerland was in the conflict zone. Sweden, which exhibited a unit root in the combined analysis, is stationary for both Male and Female when decomposed. This finding suggests that the aggregation of sexes can introduce spurious non-stationarity.

\section{Methodology}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Constrained Loss Function}

The AINN training loss combines the standard prediction error with differentiable actuarial penalty terms:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ainn_loss}
    \mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n} \| \mathbf{y}_n - \hat{\mathbf{y}}_n \|^2}_{\text{MSE}} + \lambda_1 \underbrace{\frac{1}{6}\sum_{i=1}^{6} (\hat{\Delta k}_{t,i})^2}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{coherence}}} + \lambda_2 \underbrace{\text{mean}\left(\text{ReLU}(\hat{\Delta K}_t)^2\right)}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{monotonicity}}}
\end{equation}

The coherence penalty discourages divergence of country-specific factors from the common trend (the Li-Lee assumption as a soft regularizer). The monotonicity penalty discourages positive $\Delta K_t$ (mortality worsening over time), encoding the biological prior that mortality improves in developed countries.

A stationarity penalty ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{stat}} = \text{mean}(|\hat{\Delta k}_{t,i}|)$) was tested and excluded: it consistently worsened RMSE (+0.8\% at $\lambda_3 = 0.1$, +7.9\% at $\lambda_3 = 1.0$), consistent with the empirical finding that 4 of 6 countries exhibit non-stationary specific factors.

\subsection{Joint Male/Female Training}

A single LSTM is trained on concatenated Male and Female sequences, with a binary sex indicator ($s = 0$ for Male, $s = 1$ for Female) as the 8th input feature. This doubles the effective sample size from 45 to 90 training sequences (with lookback 15) and allows the model to learn shared temporal structure (post-2011 deceleration, convergence dynamics) across sexes while preserving sex-specific output through the indicator.

\subsection{Architecture and Optimization}

The architecture is a stacked LSTM with Dropout:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Input}(15, 8) \to \text{LSTM}(48) \to \text{Dropout}(0.2) \to \text{LSTM}(32) \to \text{Dropout}(0.2) \to \text{Dense}(8)
\end{equation}

All hyperparameters --- lookback window, LSTM units, learning rate, and constraint weights $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ --- are optimized simultaneously in a 6-dimensional joint search using Optuna's TPE sampler \cite{optuna2019} (100 trials). The champion configuration is: lookback = 15, units = (48, 32), lr = 0.001, $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 0.001$.

A key finding from the optimization: all top-5 trials converge on lookback = 15, and the optimal architecture shifts from a bottleneck (64$\to$8 at lookback 10) to a balanced configuration (48$\to$32 at lookback 15). This \textit{lookback-architecture interaction} suggests that temporal context and model capacity should be co-optimized, not tuned sequentially.

\subsection{Ensemble and Process Noise Calibration}

To reduce sensitivity to random initialization, 5 models are trained independently (seeds: 42, 77, 256, 512, 1024) and their scaled predictions are averaged before inverse transformation. The ensemble achieves a cross-validation CV of 1.06\%.

The process noise $\sigma$ is calibrated on the model's \textit{walk-forward one-step-ahead residuals} (49 predictions per sex, years 1972--2020, using \texttt{training=False}):
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\text{residual}} = \text{std}(\Delta K_t^{\text{observed}} - \Delta K_t^{\text{predicted}})
\end{equation}

This isolates the irreducible uncertainty that the LSTM cannot explain, avoiding double-counting of the variability already captured by the network. The reduction is substantial: $\sigma_{K_t}$ drops from 5.99 to 2.80 (Male, $-53\%$) and from 9.08 to 3.64 (Female, $-60\%$).

\subsection{Credibility-Weighted MBC}

The standard MBC applies a constant bias correction. We extend it with a time-decaying credibility weight:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\Delta}_t = Z(t) \cdot \hat{\Delta}_t^{\text{LSTM}} + (1 - Z(t)) \cdot \mu_{\text{Li-Lee}}, \quad Z(t) = e^{-\alpha t}
\end{equation}

where $\alpha$ controls the rate at which credibility shifts from the neural model to the actuarial prior. This connects directly to B\"uhlmann credibility theory \cite{buhlmann1967}: at $\alpha = 0$ the LSTM receives full credibility; as $\alpha$ increases, the Li-Lee drift dominates for long horizons. The parameter $\alpha$ is interpretable as the risk manager's confidence in the neural model's ability to extrapolate beyond its training horizon.

\section{Discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{The Constraint as a Governance Dial}

The AINN framework's central contribution is making explicit a trade-off that is hidden in both classical and neural models. Li-Lee hardcodes its constraints (stationarity assumed, not tunable). An unconstrained LSTM has no structural bias. The AINN sits between the two, offering a parametric continuum where $\lambda$ is the lever. This is not a weakness --- it is a design feature. The risk manager selects $\lambda$ based on the regulatory context, not the modeler.

The constraint intensity analysis (Table \ref{tab:constraint}) reveals that the standard practice of selecting $\lambda$ by minimizing one-step RMSE (as done by Optuna) is \textit{suboptimal for regulatory applications}. The RMSE-optimal $\lambda = 0.001$ has near-zero effect on forecast stability. The stability-optimal $\lambda = 0.1$ costs only +0.84\% RMSE but reduces CI by 25\%. This disconnect between one-step accuracy and multi-step stability is a general finding with implications beyond this specific model.

\subsection{Residual-Calibrated Uncertainty}

The 53--60\% reduction in process noise $\sigma$ from historical to residual calibration is the single largest quantitative improvement in the paper. It demonstrates that in dual uncertainty frameworks, the calibration of the aleatoric component dominates the final confidence interval width --- more than the choice of architecture, constraint, or ensemble strategy. This finding generalizes: any neural mortality model using additive process noise should calibrate on model residuals, not historical variability.

\subsection{Credibility as a Bridge}

The time-varying credibility weight $Z(t) = e^{-\alpha t}$ provides a second governance parameter, orthogonal to $\lambda$. Together, $(\lambda, \alpha)$ define a 2D space of models:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $(\lambda \to 0, \alpha = 0)$: pure unconstrained LSTM.
    \item $(\lambda > 0, \alpha = 0)$: constrained LSTM (AINN best estimate).
    \item $(\lambda > 0, \alpha > 0)$: AINN with credibility decay (prudent long-term).
    \item $(\lambda \to \infty, \alpha \to \infty)$: pure Li-Lee RWD.
\end{itemize}

This parametric family unifies classical and neural approaches in a single framework, with the actuarial practitioner choosing the operating point.

\subsection{Limitations}

The constraints at the Optuna-selected intensity ($\lambda = 0.001$) have near-zero effect on all metrics. The value of the constraint framework emerges only through the post-hoc intensity analysis --- it was not discovered during model selection. A principled approach would optimize $\lambda$ for multi-step stability rather than one-step RMSE, but this requires a differentiable multi-step loss, which is left to future work.

The 10-year backtest shows that the AINN does not dramatically outperform Li-Lee on point accuracy (MAE 0.379 vs 0.427). The advantage is in uncertainty calibration, not prediction. For applications where only point estimates matter, the added complexity of the AINN may not be justified.

The credibility parameter $\alpha$ has no data-driven selection criterion --- it is another dial for the practitioner to turn. A future extension could derive $\alpha$ from the model's forecast variance, creating a self-calibrating credibility framework.

The monotonicity constraint is a temporal surrogate ($\Delta K_t > 0$), not the true Gompertz condition ($m_{x+1} \geq m_x$). Isotonic regression is applied post-hoc to correct data-inherited violations, but embedding the age-monotonicity constraint directly in the training loss remains future work.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% ============================================================

This paper introduces Actuarial-Informed Neural Networks (AINN), a constrained deep learning framework for multi-population longevity forecasting that makes the governance-accuracy trade-off explicit and tunable. The constraint intensity $\lambda$ is not a fixed architectural choice but a parametric dial that the risk manager sets based on the regulatory context --- from best-estimate pricing to prudent capital calibration.

Three additional methodological contributions complete the framework: residual-calibrated process noise (eliminating double-counting, CI $-$53--60\%); 5-seed model averaging (CV 1.06\%); and time-varying credibility MBC (connecting to B\"uhlmann theory). Together with the constraint analysis, these innovations produce a regulatory-grade model suite validated by a 10-year out-of-sample backtest on real HMD data.

The AINN does not claim to replace classical actuarial models. It extends them by adding two capabilities they lack: tunable structural constraints and precisely calibrated uncertainty. In a regulatory environment where model governance is as important as model accuracy, this combination fills a gap between the fully constrained world of Li-Lee and the fully unconstrained world of standard neural networks.
